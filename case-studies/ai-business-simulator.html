✦ 01 · Context

The case method, minus the on-ramp

Business schools teach through cases. For a first-year student who has not picked a major yet, a twenty-page case and the instruction “write a recommendation” is a wall, not a door.

Introductory business courses use case studies to show what strategy, finance, marketing and operations feel like in practice. The format assumes skills the audience does not have yet: how to interrogate a business problem, how to tell evidence from noise, how to build a recommendation under uncertainty. Freshmen read the whole case, feel lost, and either guess or outsource the thinking.

Instructors have the mirror-image problem. They can only see the output. With AI tools a student can summarise the reading and generate a plausible report without ever engaging with the material, and a grader cannot tell the difference. The process, which is the thing worth teaching, is invisible.

Our brief for the Gemma 4 Good Hackathon was to turn a case into something you do: an investigation with a moderator, stakeholders who hold different pieces of the truth, and a record of how the student got to their answer. We built it for two people.

Primary

The first-year student

In an intro course, undecided about a major, has never run a case. Needs a path through the problem and feedback on how they investigated, not just whether they were right.

Secondary

The instructor

Has a syllabus and their own case materials. Needs to author without a developer, publish to a class, and see each student's inquiry path, blind spots and reasoning rather than a final PDF.

✦ 03 · Key decisions

Four calls that shaped the product

Each of these cost us something. I have tried to be honest about what, and why we paid it anyway.

  1. One moderator, not a free-for-all

    The call
    Every student goes through a CEO agent that briefs the mission, decides which stakeholders are open, and judges the report. You cannot interview a stakeholder the CEO has not activated; you get sent back. Five missions, in sequence, then the final answer.
    The trade-off
    Less freedom. A blocked stakeholder is a visible “no”, and some students will find the gating restrictive when they already know who they want to talk to.
    Why
    Without a spine, an open chat with five role-play bots is just ChatGPT with costumes: students skip to “what's the answer?”. The mission structure is also what makes the process gradeable. Mission completion is 60 of the 100 points, so the structure is the assessment.

  2. Hide the answer in layers, not in instructions

    The call
    The generated playbook splits each case into small facts (“info atoms”) with access levels. L1 is free. L2 opens after you have interviewed at least one other role, L3 after two, and each atom can carry a natural-language unlock condition. Locked text never enters a prompt. The model cannot leak what it does not have.
    The trade-off
    Agents sometimes have to deflect, which can read as friction or as a bug. Instructors get a richer but more complex artefact to review.
    Why
    “Please don't reveal X” is not a boundary; students will pry it open in three messages. Layering is what turns an interview into an investigation: the CFO will not discuss cash-flow risk until you have heard Operations describe the launch plan. In the interface I turned the boundary into a feature with the “You can ask about” chips, so a deflection reads as character, not as failure.

  3. Instructors author from their own materials

    The call
    No fixed catalogue. An instructor uploads or pastes their case (PDF, Markdown, text, optionally a spreadsheet), picks a case type and difficulty, names the teaching goals, and the system generates a playbook: roles, information boundaries, missions, final questions and a scoring rubric. They review and edit before publishing.
    The trade-off
    Generation quality varies, the review step adds time, and the form has to explain what is about to be generated to someone who has never heard of a “playbook”.
    Why
    Adoption. Instructors keep the syllabus and the cases they already trust; we fit into a course instead of asking for a new one. For a hackathon framed around social good, something a teacher can use next semester mattered more than a prettier demo.

  4. Score the process, not just the answer

    The call
    100 points: 60 for mission completion, 40 for the final reflection, scored against the rubric. The debrief replays the interview path, lists the evidence found and the evidence missed, and comments on question quality.
    The trade-off
    Rubric scoring by a model is a judgement, not a measurement, and the heuristics are gameable: a “report” is detected by length and phrasing, and a confident paragraph can look like an analysis.
    Why
    The point of the product is to make inquiry visible. For the student, feedback on how they investigated is the lesson. For the instructor, a process record is the answer to AI-written reports.
✦ 04 · Multi-agent interaction design

A moderator, five stakeholders, one student

The hard design problem was not any single agent. It was making a conversation with six AI characters feel like one coherent place with rules you can learn.

The model I landed on is a loop with a host. The CEO holds the script. Stakeholders hold the facts. The student moves between them, and every move is visible in the interface.

  1. 01BriefThe CEO sets the task, names who to see and what to bring back.
  2. 02InvestigateInterview the activated stakeholders. Evidence is extracted to the board as you go.
  3. 03ReportSummarise findings and a position to the CEO.
  4. 04VerdictChecked against the evidence actually collected. Advance, or go back with a reason.
  5. 05DebriefAfter five missions: final answers with citations, scored, with blind spots.

↻ Steps 01–04 repeat × 5 missions

The CEO has three modes, and never asks questions

A moderator that chats is a moderator that gets argued with. So the CEO only ever does one of three things, and the interface makes the current one legible.

Briefing

Opens a mission: the deliverable, the stakeholders it activates, the handoff text pinned in the right rail.

Evaluating

Triggered when a message reads like a report. Issues a verdict grounded in the evidence board. Advancement is fail-closed: no explicit “complete”, no progress.

Redirecting

Everything else. Points the student back to the mission instead of answering for them.

Anatomy of the interview room

  1. Stakeholder railThe CEO is labelled as the orchestrator and sits apart. Activated stakeholders are live; the rest stay listed so the student can see what is coming.
  2. Agent card with a remitA one-line persona and “You can ask about” chips. The chips are the public edge of the information boundary: they make a good first question obvious and make a deflection feel in character.
  3. The threadReplies stream in with a thinking indicator timed to the unlock check, so a pause reads as the character considering rather than the app stalling.
  4. Roadmap and handoff“Mission 1 of 5”, the current brief, and “Report back to CEO when done”. Orientation is persistent, because the most common failure in multi-agent chat is not knowing what you are supposed to be doing.

Agents nudge instead of lecture

Each stakeholder picks a follow-up strategy per turn from an ordered list, and the first one that applies wins: validate what the student just said, probe toward a locked fact, pose a calculation challenge, check an item off the mission checklist, refer the student to another role, or simply go deeper. Used prompts are remembered so the same nudge does not repeat. The effect is a conversation that keeps moving the student toward evidence without handing it over.

✦ 05 · Iteration

What changed between the first demo and the last

The repository shows the shape of the iteration: an orchestration overhaul in the middle, then a run of changes that chased trust and latency. What it does not show is the observation behind each change, which is the part I need to add.

  1. Structure

    From open chat to missions

    The orchestration overhaul replaced free conversation with the CEO-moderated mission loop and the redesigned interview room. [NEED: what the pre-mission version looked like and the moment that convinced us to add structure]

  2. Trust

    Verdicts grounded in collected evidence

    The CEO's judgement was re-anchored to the evidence board rather than to the student's own report. A report that merely sounded complete could no longer pass. [NEED: confirm the trigger, e.g. a test session where a student bluffed through a mission]

  3. Latency

    Reply first, extract later

    Agent replies now return and stream immediately; evidence extraction, board updates and checklist evaluation run in the background with a per-session lock so concurrent writes do not collide. The thinking indicator was re-timed to the unlock check.

  4. Instructor side

    Analytics and citations

    Per-case analytics on the instructor dashboard and a citation flow for final answers, so a submission points at the evidence it rests on.

  5. Positioning

    Three names in a week

    CaseIQ became BizSimAI, then AI Business Decision Simulation. [NEED: why each rename, and which name we'd keep]

✦ 06 · Outcome

Where it landed

We shipped a working, deployed platform with the whole loop in it, submitted it to the hackathon's main, impact and prize tracks, and open-sourced it under Apache 2.0.

  • End-to-end loop: upload a case, generate and review a playbook, publish, run five missions, receive a scored debrief.
  • Five stakeholder role types with persona routing, information layering and per-turn guide strategies.
  • CEO orchestrator with briefing, evaluating and redirecting modes and a fail-closed verdict.
  • Streaming replies, background evidence extraction and an evidence board with citations.
  • Instructor authoring from PDF, Markdown, text and spreadsheets, plus per-case analytics.
  • Live demo on Vercel, FastAPI backend, Supabase storage, Gemma 4 models via Google AI Studio.

✦ 07 · What I'd do next

What I'd do next

  • Make the evidence board a workspace. Today it is filled in the background. I want students to pull quotes onto claims, see what is missing per mission, and cite from the board in their final answer without leaving it.
  • Show instructors the layers. The playbook review step edits lists. A visual map of which facts sit behind which interviews would let a teacher tune difficulty by moving an atom, not by rewriting text.
  • Run a real pilot and measure learning. Question quality across missions, time to first evidence, blind-spot rate. The scoring dimensions exist; they have not been validated against outcomes yet.
  • A phone layout for the interview room. The three-column screen is desktop-first. The roadmap and handoff should become a bottom sheet, and the stakeholder rail a horizontal strip.
  • Go up the curriculum. Upper-level courses and professional training want deeper finance and operations models, and multi-session cases that remember what the student decided last week.
